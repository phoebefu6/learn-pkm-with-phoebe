PARA, and one home per note

Your ten notes now have titles somebody could search for, and three of them still could not be found. That is not a titling failure. A title decides whether a note answers a search; where the note lives decides whether you ever pass it when you are not searching at all. Tonight is the second decision. Four categories, one question that picks between them in about two seconds, and a rule about copies that saves more arguments than any of it. Plus the part that is usually left out: what filing genuinely buys you, and what it cannot do no matter how neat you make it.

Part 0

Where we start

Three of your ten came back from last session with titles that are genuinely good, and they still could not be found. Nothing is wrong with them. A good title makes a note searchable, and there is nothing a title can do to make a note reachable when you are not already searching for it. Reachable is a different property with a different cause, and the cause is where the note lives. At Daybreak, the coffee subscription company, that means the same forty-eight notes and a real shape to sort them into: two live projects, the autumn roast launch and subscription retention; three ongoing areas, suppliers, the roastery and the team; a reference shelf of saved reading and how-we-do-things; and an archive of work that is finished.

Part 1 · covers the four categories

Projects, areas, resources, archive

5 min live

Four categories, and they are not four topics. The thing that makes them work is that none of them is a subject. You are not asked what the note is about, which is a question with as many answers as you have moods. You are asked what you would be doing when you next need it, and that has one answer.

LiveThe four, in Forte's own words3 min▶

Worth having the original wording rather than a paraphrase, because the paraphrases quietly turn them back into subjects.

  • Projects are "projects you're actively working on - short-term efforts (in your work or personal life) that you take on with a certain goal in mind".
  • Areas are "areas of responsibility - important parts of your work and life that require ongoing attention".
  • Resources are "resources on a range of topics you're interested in and learning about".
  • Archives are "anything from the previous three categories that is no longer active, but you might want to save for future reference".

The distinction that does all the work is in the first two. A project has a finish line and an area does not. "Ship the autumn roast" ends: there is a day when it is done and you would know. "Suppliers" does not end; you will still have suppliers next year and the year after, and there is no version of Tuesday where you tick it off. Everything else about the four categories is bookkeeping around that one difference.

The fourth category deserves a word, because people skip it. Archive is not deletion and it is not a graveyard. It is the move that keeps the first three small enough to look at, which is the only reason they work at all.

Self-studyOrganize by actionability, and what it is arguing against4 min▶

Forte's mantra for the whole scheme is three words: "organize by actionability". He sets it explicitly against organizing "by broad subjects like in school" - the instinct that files everything under Psychology, or Marketing, or Coffee, the way a library would.

The reason that instinct fails is not that subjects are wrong, it is that they are ambiguous at exactly the wrong moment. A note about a supplier's pricing for the autumn roast is about pricing, and suppliers, and the launch, and coffee. Four correct answers, and you will pick a different one on a different day, which means next year you will look in the wrong one. Actionability has a single answer because it is a question about you rather than about the note: what would you be doing when you need this? If the answer is "shipping the autumn roast", the note goes with the autumn roast, and it does not matter that it is also about pricing.

Forte's own origin story for PARA is the mistake in its purest form, and it is worth retelling because everybody makes it. He was working with a Senior Director at a biotech company who produced a project list, and the list turned out to be nothing of the kind: Hiring, Strategic Planning, Vacations. Not one of those items had a finish line, so not one of them was a project, and a list of things that can never be completed is a list that can only make you feel behind.

One caution, since it is the honest one. Whether actionability-based filing is genuinely easier to maintain than topic-based filing is asserted by its author, not measured. No controlled comparison of the two on real people's maintenance behaviour was found. What can be said without stretching anything is narrower and still useful: actionability gives a note one answer where a subject gives it four, and a question with one answer is the kind you can still answer in a year.

Part 2 · covers one home

One home, and a pointer if it is needed

6 min live

Every filing session hits the same wall within ten minutes: a note that belongs in two places. The instinct is to put it in both, and the instinct is wrong for a reason that has nothing to do with tidiness.

LiveWhy a note gets exactly one home4 min▶

Put a note in two places and for about a week you have one note in two convenient locations, which feels like a clever solution to a real problem. Then somebody edits one of them. From that moment you do not have one note in two places, you have two different notes with the same title, and the expensive part is not the duplication. It is that neither one is trustworthy any more. You cannot tell by looking which is current, so the only safe move is to open both and compare, every time, forever. That is a worse position than having filed it in the wrong place, because a note in the wrong place is at least still correct.

The fix is small: one home, plus a pointer from anywhere else that has a claim on it. A pointer is a line of text with a link in it, and it cannot drift, because there is nothing in it to drift. It says where the thing lives, and when the thing changes the pointer keeps being right.

Which leaves the actual question: which of the two places is the home? The same rule as everything else tonight. Where would you be standing when you next need it? The legal sign-off on the origin claim will be needed when somebody is shipping the launch, because that is when a claim has to be defensible. So the launch project is the home, and the suppliers area gets the pointer. Not because packaging matters less, but because nobody goes looking in an area when they are chasing a deadline.

This is the whole of the actionability rule applied to one decision, and it is why the rule is worth having: it turns a judgement call that could absorb ten minutes of a meeting into a question with one answer.

Self-studyWhat filing does not fix5 min▶

Two things that are commonly claimed about filing are not supported, and it is better to know that now than to discover it in month six.

The first is that folders beat tags. Bergman and colleagues, in 2008, put both models on comparable sets of the same information and followed how people kept, organized and re-found things with each. The reported conclusion is that "each model has its strengths and weaknesses". No clean winner. The paper's own framing rejects a simple "tags beat folders" and rejects the reverse just as firmly, and that is a finding from the literature rather than a shrug, so it is worth stating as one. The tier here is honest too: this is the publisher's indexed abstract, read directly, not the full paper.

The second is more specific and comes up whenever somebody defends folders. There is no study quantifying a point at which having too many tags starts to hurt you. A targeted search for it turned up related work on tagging inconsistency, and on how a tag's own attributes affect browsing, but nothing that measures a proliferation threshold or an over-tagging penalty as an effect in its own right. So the sentence "past about thirty tags you slow down" has no source behind it, and neither does any variant with a number in it. Say instead what is true, which is that nobody has measured this.

What filing genuinely does is narrow and real: it puts a note within reach of somewhere you already go. That is the entire benefit, and it is worth the twenty minutes. What it cannot do is make a note reachable that nothing points at. A note can have a perfect home, in the correct category, spelled correctly, and if no route runs through that home in your ordinary week then filing it changed nothing about your chances of seeing it again. Session 4 measures exactly that difference on a real collection, and the gap between those two states is larger than almost anyone expects.

Real world

At Daybreak the legal sign-off on the origin claim genuinely belonged to Packaging and to Comms, so it was kept in both, and for five weeks nothing went wrong. Then the sleeve team edited their copy after legal loosened the wording, and three days later the launch owner read the other one and wrote the old wording into the campaign brief. Nobody was careless. Both of them opened a note called "Origin claim - legal sign-off", in a sensible folder, and read it.

Part 3 · covers the limits

A filing cabinet is not a finding

4 min live

Here is the part that gets left out of every version of this talk, including the enthusiastic ones. You can file a collection perfectly and still not be able to get at most of it, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of effort.

LiveFiling puts things away; links bring them back4 min▶

A category is an instruction for putting a note down. That is what it is for and it does it well. But notice what it does not contain: nothing about a category creates a route back to the note later. Sitting in Resources does not make a note pass in front of you. It only means that if you ever decide to go and look in Resources, it will be there.

Routes come from somewhere else entirely. They come from the two or three notes you actually open every week - the live project, the thing on your screen right now - pointing at the things they depend on. That is the whole mechanism. Your launch note links to the supplier contract, the legal sign-off and the pricing sheet, and now those three are one click from a place you are standing anyway. You did not tidy them. You made them adjacent to your Tuesday.

Both pictures above have the same homes. The only difference is a handful of links out of the two notes the person opens anyway, and the second picture puts most of the collection within two steps of somewhere they already are. Session 4 measures exactly this on a real collection, and the difference the links make is larger than nearly everybody expects when they guess first. Guess your number tonight, before you see it.

Self-studyPARA's own author on PARA's limits4 min▶

The best version of this argument is not a critique from outside. It is Forte's own. In his essay on progressive summarization, he describes PARA on its own as "a notebook-first system" and calls it "woefully inadequate" without design at the level of the individual note sitting on top of it. His whole argument in that piece is that neither tagging-first nor notebook-first carries the weight, and that the note itself has to be built to be found.

That is a striking thing for an author to say about the method he is best known for, and it is the right way to read tonight. Filing is a floor, not a system. It gets a note out of the pile and into a place with a name, and past that it has nothing further to offer you.

The second quote is the one that should stop anybody from waiting until they can do this properly. His myth number two, in his own words: a second brain is "adaptive", and "any incremental improvement immediately makes a difference, whether or not the other parts of the system are already in place". Partial adoption is not a failed implementation, it is the normal case. Ten notes filed tonight is not a tenth of a system that will start working when you finish; it is ten notes you can now get to. Nobody ever has a complete anything, and it does not matter.

Build-along

File the ten

★ 22 min · the same ten notes from last week

The ten retitled notes, and nothing else. The rule for the next twenty minutes: no new categories. Four is the whole vocabulary. Every time somebody invents a fifth, it is because they are trying to file by subject again, and the fifth one always turns out to be a topic.

Write the projects with finish lines. Not what you are busy with, what has an end. Give each one the finish line in the same line: "Autumn roast launch - shipped when the first box goes out." If you cannot write the second half of that sentence, it is not a project, and you have just found something useful.

Write the areas that never finish. Suppliers. The roastery. The team. Your own health, if that is a thing you keep notes about. The test is the same one backwards: if there is no day on which somebody could say "that is done", it goes here. Most people's lists come out around three to six.

Take each of the ten and ask which of the four. Do not ask what it is about. Ask what you would be doing when you next need it, and file it where you would be standing. If the answer is "nothing, I would just be curious", that is Resources and that is a real answer. If the answer is "I would be looking back at something finished", that is Archive.

Find the one that wants two homes. There is almost always exactly one, and you will know it because it is the one you have been leaving until last. Pick its home with the same question, not by importance: where would you be standing when you need it? Deadlines beat responsibilities, so a project beats an area nearly every time.

Add the pointer from the other place. One line, with a link in it, that says where the note lives. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the reason the other half of the argument does not need to be had again in six weeks.

Do the project list before you touch a single note. Filing into categories you have not written down yet is how people end up with eleven projects and no areas. Five minutes of listing saves the whole exercise.

Say the finish line out loud. "Done when the first box ships." "Done when the retention number is reported to the board." A finish line you cannot say is a finish line that does not exist, and the item is an area.

One home each, no exceptions tonight. The moment you allow yourself a second copy you will allow yourself a fourth, and in three months nothing in the collection can be trusted without opening two of it.

When you genuinely cannot decide, file it under the project. Projects end, and when this one ends you will look at its contents again on the way to the archive. An area never gives you that second look, so a wrong guess costs less inside a project.

Archive without ceremony. If something on your list finished last quarter, move it now, in one action, whole. It is the cheapest way to make the other three categories small enough to actually look at.

Keep the ten together. Session 4 runs a bench and session 5 puts them in a review. Same ten notes, all course, which is the only way you will be able to tell what changed.

Two rules of thumb that settle most stuck decisions. A note you cannot place is almost always a resource - you saved it because it was interesting, not because anything is waiting on it. And a project you cannot finish is almost always an area wearing a project's name, which is exactly the biotech Senior Director's list, and moving it is a relief rather than a demotion.

Homework

Before session 4

25 min
  1. File one week of your inbox using the four categories, and time it. Start a timer, work through whatever accumulated since last week, and write down the minutes at the end. The number matters because a filing habit that costs twenty minutes a week is one you will drop, and if that is your number the fix is fewer categories rather than more discipline.
  2. Move anything from your project list that has no finish line into areas, and count how many moved. Most people move several, and a few move most of the list. The count is worth having because it tells you how much of what felt like being behind was just a category error.
  3. Pick your two most-opened notes. Not the two most important, the two you actually opened most in the last month. Session 4 uses the places you really go as its starting points, and if you bring the wrong two the numbers you get will be about somebody else's week.
  4. Bring a laptop. Session 4 is a bench, not a talk. One collection, filed five different ways, asked sixteen real questions eight months after the notes were written, and you will want to be able to run it yourself rather than watch.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Somebody hands you a list headed "My projects": Hiring, Strategic Planning, Vacations. What is wrong with it?

This is Forte's own origin anecdote, a biotech Senior Director whose project list turned out to be a list of areas. The single distinction that carries the whole scheme is that a project has a finish line and an area does not. Moving those three is not a demotion, it is the thing that makes the remaining project list mean something.

2 · A note genuinely belongs to two places. Why one home plus a pointer rather than a copy in each?

The cost is reliability, not neatness. A note filed in the wrong place is at least still correct, whereas two copies that have diverged leave you unable to tell by looking which one is current. The home is chosen by the same question as everything else: where would you be standing when you next need it.

3 · What does filing a collection well actually do for you, and what does it not do?

Bergman and colleagues in 2008 compared both models on comparable information over time and report that each has its strengths and weaknesses, with no winner declared. A targeted search for a measured over-tagging threshold found nothing, so any sentence with a number in it about tag proliferation is invented. What filing does is make a note adjacent to a place you already stand, and session 4 measures how much of a collection that leaves out of reach.
