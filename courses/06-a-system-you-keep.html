A system you keep

Every note system works in a good week. You have time, the inbox is short, filing feels satisfying. The question that decides whether any of this was worth doing is what happens in the other kind of week: the one with two deadlines and a sick child, where the inbox reaches forty and you have not opened a project note since Monday. Systems are not abandoned in bad weeks. They are abandoned in the good week afterwards, when the backlog is too big to face. Tonight is the version that survives that, the way back when it does not, and an honest score on what you actually have.

Part 0

Where we start

You have a capture rule, titles that carry their search words, one home per note, thirty-something links where there were none, and a weekly pass. That is a complete loop, and complete loops are fragile in a specific way: every part assumes the part before it happened. Tonight is about which parts are load-bearing and which are luxuries, because that is the difference between a system you have and a system you keep.

Part 1 · covers the floor

Two moves, and everything else is optional

5 min live

Design the bad week deliberately, in advance, and write it down. A floor you chose is a floor you return to; a floor you fell through is an abandoned system.

LiveWhy those two and not the others3 min▶

The test is simple: what is lost forever if you skip this move for two weeks?

Capture and title are load-bearing because they are the only two that happen at a moment which does not come back. The supplier said the thing once. If it was never captured, no amount of filing next month recovers it, and if it was captured as "Notes", the bench showed exactly what happens: a note with no searchable word and nothing pointing at it is lost in all five filings, permanently and quietly.

Filing, linking and reviewing are deferrable because the information still exists and the work merely accumulates. An inbox of forty is an afternoon. It is unpleasant, it is not a loss. The distinction matters because guilt attaches to the visible backlog, which is the cheap part, and never to the untitled capture, which is the expensive one.

So the floor is two moves and about six seconds per item. Write it down as a sentence you can read on a bad Tuesday: everything goes in one place, and everything gets a real title.

Self-studyPartial systems are the normal case3 min▶

The author of the best-known method says this himself, and it is his second listed myth about his own work: the system is "adaptive", and "any incremental improvement immediately makes a difference, whether or not the other parts of the system are already in place." That is not encouragement, it is a structural claim, and the bench supports it. Going from one folder to PARA moved the lost count from four to two without a single link being added. Adding links moved reachability from 38 percent to 73 percent without changing a single folder. Each move paid on its own.

The practical consequence is that "I am behind on my system" is usually a category error. You are behind on the deferrable half. The half that cannot be caught up on is the half you did at the time, and if you captured and titled, you are not behind at all, you have a queue.

Part 2 · covers the way back

Coming back after a month away

4 min live

It will happen. A project takes over, or you are ill, or it is December. You open the inbox in January and there are ninety things in it. The instinct is to start at the top and file carefully, which takes three hours, so instead you close it and the system is over. There is a better procedure and it takes about twenty minutes.

LiveThe amnesty, and why guilt is pointed at the wrong thing3 min▶

A month-old inbox item that you have not needed in a month is, empirically, an item you did not need. Archiving it costs nothing that was not already gone: it keeps its title, it stays searchable, and it sits exactly where an unread inbox item sat. The only thing you lose is the feeling that you were going to get to it.

What the digital hoarding research measures is relevant here and worth stating carefully. Neave and colleagues found "difficulty deleting" to be a real, measurable trait across two samples, correlating with how much people accumulate at work. It is not a character failing and the qualitative work refuses the pathology framing, finding a spectrum from hoarding to deliberate minimalism with most people in between. But a collection with no discard step only ever travels one way, and a person who finds discarding hard needs the discard to be a scheduled, low-stakes, reversible move rather than a judgement call made ninety times in an afternoon. That is precisely what an archive is.

Self-studyWhat to do if the collection has outgrown you entirely3 min▶

Sometimes it is not a month, it is three years and eleven thousand items across five tools. The instinct is a migration project. Do not do the migration project; they fail, and they fail late, after a lot of weekends.

Do this instead. Declare the old collection an archive, entire and untouched, and leave it exactly where it is. It stays searchable, which is the only thing it was ever reliably giving you. Start a new place with the two floor moves, and let the archive be the thing you search when the new place does not have something. Within a couple of months the new place holds everything current, the archive holds the past, and you have spent no weekends. The cost is that the old collection never becomes navigable, and the bench is clear-eyed about what that means: it will stay at search-only retrieval forever. That is a real loss, and it is smaller than the migration you were considering.

Real world

The Daybreak person had three years of notes in an app they had stopped liking. They exported it, put the whole export in one archive folder, and started clean. Eight months on they had searched the archive four times and found what they wanted twice. Both of the failures were notes titled "Notes".

Part 3 · covers knowing it worked

The only number worth tracking

3 min live

Not how many notes you have, which measures the wrong thing and goes up regardless. Not how tidy it looks. The number is the one you measured in session 1, before any of this: out of ten notes sampled at random, how many could you actually get back?

LiveRe-run the session 1 audit, now3 min▶

Same method, so the comparison means something. Scroll to a random point, take ten notes in a row, and ask the three questions of each: could somebody search for this by its title alone, does anything link to it or sit beside it in a place small enough to scan, and was it ever going to be reread. Two yeses or more is findable.

Compare with the number you wrote down in session 1. Be careful about what the comparison proves: the notes you have captured since are the ones made under the new rules, and the old ones have mostly not been touched, so an improvement is partly a measure of how much you have added recently. That is a real limitation of measuring yourself, and it is still the most honest signal available. If the number has not moved at all, the likeliest cause is that the sample is dominated by old notes, and the second likeliest is that you have been filing rather than linking.

Build-along

Set the floor, then score it

★ 22 min · your real collection

Twelve minutes writing the two things you will actually do when the week goes badly, then ten scoring what you have against everything the course claimed.

Write the floor. Two sentences, present tense, naming the place and the rule: "Everything goes in the one inbox. Everything gets a title with the word I would search for." Put it at the top of the inbox itself, where you cannot miss it.

Write the way back. Four lines: amnesty everything older than a month, file only live-project items, retitle only those, restart the weekly pass. Put it directly under the floor.

Re-run the audit. Ten random notes, three questions each, same as session 1. Write today's number next to the old one, with the date.

Put the pass in the calendar if it is not there, recurring, fifteen minutes, at a time you are actually at your desk and not at the end of a Friday when you are leaving.

Score the card below honestly, including the rows you know you have not done. A scorecard you flatter tells you nothing you did not already believe.

If you take one thing from the whole course. A collection is not what you have saved, it is what you can get back, and the two moves that decide it both happen in the four seconds when the thing arrives. Everything after that is recoverable work. Everything in those four seconds is not.

Homework

After the course

25 min
  1. Run the floor for two weeks deliberately, doing nothing else: capture and title only, no filing, no linking. Then spend one afternoon on the backlog and notice how little was actually lost. That experiment is what makes the floor believable when you need it.
  2. Re-run the ten-note audit in three months and again in six. Three data points with dates is the only evidence any of this worked, and it costs ten minutes each time.
  3. Add ten more links from the notes you open most. It remains the cheapest change available and it is the one nobody does, because filing feels like the work and linking feels like a detour.
  4. Find one more "Interesting". Every collection has them. Retitle it and link it from somewhere you go, and each one you fix is a question you will be able to answer in a year.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why are capture and titling the floor, rather than filing?

The test is what is lost forever if you skip the move for two weeks. Filing, linking and reviewing lose nothing permanently, they just pile up. The bench showed what an untitled, unlinked note costs: it is lost in all five filings, and no later tidying recovers it.

2 · You open the inbox after a month away and there are ninety items. What is the right first move?

Archiving is not deleting: those items keep their titles and stay searchable, which is exactly the position they were in sitting unread. Careful filing of ninety items takes three hours, and three hours is how people decide never to open the system again.

3 · Which number tells you whether the collection is working?

Note count rises whatever happens, including while the system fails. Inbox zero measures the deferrable half. Tags measure effort, and the bench put 192 tag decisions against zero additional answers. The sampled-ten number is the only one that measures what a collection was for.
