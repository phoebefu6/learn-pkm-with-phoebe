The retrieval bench

Three sessions of rules, and tonight they get measured. One collection of forty-eight notes goes through five filings, from a single folder to PARA with links, and after each one a bench asks the collection sixteen questions the way a person actually asks them eight months later. Not "is this tidy". Can you get the thing back? Every answer lands in one of three buckets: found by looking, found by searching, or gone.

Part 0

Where we start

Sessions 2 and 3 gave you rules: a title carrying the word you would search for, one home per note, filed by what you would be doing when you next need it. Rules are easy to agree with and hard to know you have followed. A bench is a rule you cannot nod at, because it reports a number and the number is either what you hoped or it is not.

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class

★ What you walk out with tonight. The five filings in order with their numbers, the knowledge of which one your own collection is currently in, and the two moves that actually change the count, which are not the two most people spend their weekend on.

The collection is constructed and the measurement is real. The forty-eight notes were written to be realistic, not sampled from anyone's actual collection, because no published figures exist on how big real collections are, how many notes are orphans, or how densely people link them. That was searched for and it is not there. So every number below describes this collection only. What is real is the machinery: the traversal, the folder walk and the keyword matching all genuinely run, and the thresholds they use are stated on the widget so you can argue with them.

Part 1 · covers the three buckets

Found by looking, found by searching, gone

6 min live

A question asked eight months later has exactly three fates, and the third one is the only number that matters. Everything the bench measures feeds into deciding which bucket a question lands in.

LiveThe four thresholds, and why each is what it is4 min▶

Every model has numbers baked into it, and a bench that hides them is asking to be believed rather than checked. These four are on the widget.

  • Two hops. Navigation starts at the places you actually open each week, not at the top of the tree, and runs two steps. Nobody clicks further than that for a note they half-remember.
  • Twelve notes is a scannable place. Past about a dozen you are not looking any more, you are reading a list, and lists are where notes go to be forgotten. This is the single most consequential threshold in the bench: it is why one big folder does not count as somewhere you can find things.
  • A useful tag points at eight notes or fewer. A tag sitting on twenty notes returns twenty notes, which is a list, not an answer.
  • A title is searchable if a query word is in it. A note called "Notes" is not findable by search, whatever is inside it, because there is no word to match.

Disagree with any of them and the widget still works; the numbers move and the ordering of the five filings does not. That is worth knowing, because it means the lesson does not depend on the thresholds being exactly right.

Self-studyWhy navigation is scored before search3 min▶

Because that is what people do. Bergman and colleagues compared users of better and worse desktop search tools and found people estimated they used navigation, meaning browsing through folders, for 56 to 68 percent of file retrievals, and search for only 4 to 15 percent. Improving the quality of the search engine had a "limited and inconsistent" effect on how often people actually searched, and search was used "mainly as a last resort when users could not remember file location". These are self-reported estimates from questionnaires rather than logged behaviour, which is a real limitation and worth carrying with the number.

The consequence for a collection is direct. If most retrieval is navigation, then a note that navigation cannot reach is already relying on the fallback, and if its title has no searchable word the fallback fails too. That is not two independent problems. It is one problem with two locks, and session 2 unlocked one of them.

The bench

Forty-eight notes, five filings, sixteen questions

★ 26 min · everyone drives

Take the filings in order and read the three bucket counts before you read anything else. The same forty-eight notes are in every one; only where they sit, what points at them and what is tagged changes.

The retrieval bench

One collection, organised five ways. Every number is computed from the graph: a real traversal over real links, a real folder walk, and a real keyword match against each note's own title and tags.

The ladder below was read off this bench in a browser before this page was written. Take the filings in the same order and you will get the same numbers, because they come from the same measurement.

LiveRead the ladder: the step that feels like progress5 min▶
  • One big folder finds nothing by looking. Zero of sixteen. Every question that gets answered is answered by search, and the four that search cannot answer are gone. The folder holds all forty-eight notes, which is why it reaches nothing: you cannot look in a place that size, you can only scroll it.
  • Sorting into topic folders is the honest disappointment. Reachability triples, from 4 percent to 23 percent, and the lost count does not move at all. Still four. This is the weekend everybody spends: the collection becomes tidy, it photographs well, and the questions you could not answer on Friday you still cannot answer on Monday.
  • PARA halves the lost count, from four to two, because filing by what you are doing puts the project material next to the two notes you actually open. Nine of sixteen now answer by looking. Note what did it: not better categories in the abstract, but categories that sit where your attention already is.
  • Links are the move. Adding the links a person would naturally make, from the project notes to the things they depend on, takes it to fourteen of sixteen by looking and 73 percent reachable. Thirty-seven links. That is an afternoon, and it does more than the entire filing exercise before it.
  • Tagging everything is the trap. One hundred and ninety-two filing decisions, four tags on every note, and the buckets are identical: fourteen, one, one. It is not that tags are harmful. It is that this work bought nothing, while feeling more like organising than anything else in the list.
LiveThe question that is lost in all five4 min▶

Read the panel under the numbers on the best filing. One question survives every improvement: "the competitor thing somebody sent me."

Its note is titled "Interesting". Nothing links to it. It sits in a perfectly correct folder under a perfectly sensible category, and there is no route to it and no word to search for. No amount of further filing recovers it, because filing was never the thing that was broken. It was captured in four seconds by a rule that said keep what resonates, given a title that meant something for about a day, and then left alone.

That single row is the argument of the whole course. A note is recoverable if something points at it or its title carries the word you will look for. A note with neither is a note you have already lost, and you lost it at the moment of capture, not at the moment of filing.

LiveNow yours: which filing are you in8 min▶

Open your own collection beside the bench and answer four questions honestly.

How big is your biggest place? If one folder, notebook or tag holds more than about a dozen notes, that place is not somewhere you find things. Count it. Most people find their biggest place holds more than half of everything they own.

What do you open every week? Two or three notes, usually. Those are your entry points, and everything you can reach in two steps from them is your real collection. Everything else is in storage.

How many links are there? Not tags, not folders: notes that point at other notes. For most people the answer is zero, which puts them on the third row at best.

Ask yourself four questions you failed to answer this year, and trace each one. Reached from an entry point? Matched by a title? Or gone? That ratio is your own bench result, and you can run it without any tooling at all.

The number to bring to session 5. Your links count, and the size of your biggest place. Those two predict almost everything else, and they are the two that filing advice almost never mentions.

Part 2 · covers honest limits

What the numbers can and cannot tell you

3 min live

A bench that only reports what it can measure has to be clear about what it cannot. Four limits, on the widget and here.

LimitWhat it means for the numbers
The collection is constructedForty-eight notes written to be realistic, not sampled. No published figures on real collection size, orphan rate or link density were found, so these numbers describe this collection and are not a claim about how anybody's vault looks.
The thresholds are judgementTwelve notes for a scannable place, eight for a useful tag, two hops for navigation. They are stated so they can be argued with. Moving them moves the numbers and leaves the ordering of the five filings unchanged.
Two entry pointsModels somebody with two live projects. A person with eight would reach more of their collection, and would also have more of it to maintain.
It measures retrieval, not valueA collection can score well and be full of notes that were never worth keeping. Nothing here says a reachable note is a useful one.
Self-studyWhat the tagging row does and does not prove3 min▶

Be careful with this one, because it is the easiest result on the page to overstate. The bench shows that in this collection, applying four tags to every note bought no additional answers. That is a measurement of a cost and a return, in one constructed corpus, under stated thresholds.

It is not a finding that tagging harms retrieval. The personal-information-management literature compared folders and tags on the same material and reports no clean winner: each model has strengths and weaknesses for keeping, organising and re-finding. And no study was found that quantifies a point at which adding tags starts to cost you, which is why no such number appears anywhere here. The defensible sentence is the narrow one: tagging is work, work should buy something, and in this collection it did not.

Real world

The Daybreak person spent a Sunday adding tags to everything and felt, correctly, that the collection had never looked better. The following Thursday they could not find the supplier call, for the same reason as before: nothing pointed at it from the launch note they actually had open.

Homework

Before session 5

25 min
  1. Add ten links. Open your two most-used notes and make each one point at the things it actually depends on. Ten is about twenty minutes and it is the highest-return twenty minutes in the course.
  2. Find your biggest place and split it if it holds more than a dozen notes. Not into a perfect taxonomy, just into places small enough to look at.
  3. Run your own four-question bench and write the ratio down: found by looking, found by searching, gone. Compare it with the ten-note audit from session 1.
  4. Find your own "Interesting". One note with a meaningless title and nothing pointing at it. Fix it now: retitle it and link it from somewhere you go, and notice that the fix takes under a minute once the note is in front of you.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sorting the collection from one folder into topic folders tripled reachability and left the lost count at four. Why?

The lost bucket is defined by having neither a route nor a searchable title. Tidying the shelves changes neither for those notes. That is why the step which feels most like progress can move a real number, reachability, while leaving the number that matters exactly where it was.

2 · One big folder holding all forty-eight notes scores zero found by looking. What is the bench modelling?

The scannable-place threshold is twelve, and it is the most consequential number in the model. It is also why splitting one huge place into several small ones does something real, even before any linking: it converts storage into places you can actually look in.

3 · Tagging all forty-eight notes cost 192 filing decisions and changed no bucket. What is the honest conclusion?

The measurement is of cost and return in one corpus, not a general law. Overstating it into "tagging is bad" would be exactly the kind of claim session 1 spent its time refusing. Tagging is work; work should buy something; here it did not.
